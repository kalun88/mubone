What the three verbs actually are
bang — one edge, no span. There is nothing to release and nothing to switch off. An impulse.
momentary — two edges, and the span between them is your finger. A spring.
toggle — two edges, and the span between them is open until you close it. A latch.

So the axis is is there a span, and is it closed. The diamond said nothing about that — it was distinct, not accurate — and a rounded square is the tile's default shape, so momentary read as an absence of information. Two replacements settle by argument: bang is a circle (a point has no duration; every struck pad in music hardware is round; it was the dot in 1a) and momentary is the plate at --r-hand, whose token note already reads "an object you grab with a whole hand". Below, the toggle three ways.

No hand. One tile, one binding, and the shape is the verb 3a the silhouette · 3b the frame · 3c where the verb is set
What dies with the hand

Arming was one model (pick a tool, space uses it) laid over another (a key per tool), and you play the second. So: no sel, no armed box, no radio bracket, no invariant that a tool is always on the palette, no spring-loaded hand-back, no tap-to-arm. Space becomes an ordinary binding — learned onto whichever tile you play most, and it wears the spacebar mark like any other key.

The three rows per position collapse to one. A position is no longer a thing plus a choice of how to fire it: the verb is part of what the tile is. Want pin as a bang and pin as a drawn path? Two tiles. That is your "a glyph for each binding", and it falls out rather than being added.

The palette bar — what a tile has to tell you, and how 1a the vocabulary · 1b lane · 1c rail · 1d ledger · 1e the button map
The problem, stated

A position holds one of three kinds of thing, and each kind answers a different set of verbs — verbsOf already knows this. A tool arms on tap and has both activate rows. A lens is on or off on tap and has a momentary but no toggle. A pin tile bangs on tap, and pin alone draws a path. On top of that, each verb can be reached by a key, a MIDI note or CC, an instrument button gesture, or OSC.

The tile shows a glyph, a hue and one key. So the strip is truthful about which tool and silent about how it fires — which is exactly the complaint: the momentary and the toggle look the same, and a lens reads as a tool. Three signals, three questions, and none of them may cost a second glance: shape says the kind · a mark says the verb · the chip's ground says which input.

What it buys. Every input that reaches a tile is on the tile, in one line, and the verb travels with the source rather than sitting in a corner away from it — so "B1 long is a momentary on pen" is one chip, not two facts you assemble. The bracket under the tool run is the radio group, and the ember segment is who holds the arm: the armed box says it twice, which is the one repetition worth paying for when the strip has to read from three metres.
What it costs. Tiles are no longer one width — a tile with three chips is wider than a tile with one, so the strip's rhythm comes from its content. Nothing is fixed-slot, so absence is quiet: a tile with nothing bound shows an empty lane, which reads as "not yet" rather than as a warning. Widths run 96–124px against today's 53.

What it buys. The tiles stay the objects you hit — 53px, glyph only, nothing shrunk to make room — and the ledger is a table with three fixed rows, so every cell is in the same place on every tile.
